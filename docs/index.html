FlyRank ML Internship · Lane 2 — Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring

Which pages should you refresh first?

A client-grouped, leakage-audited comparison of a transparent rule against a learned model for ranking content-refresh priority — and what happened the moment I let one leaked column back in.

Aun Mehdi · March 2026 dataset · Repo ↗
00 — Abstract

Question: out of a content portfolio too large to review page by page, which pages should a content team check first this month — and can a learned model rank them better than a transparent rule?

Data: 30,000 pages across 32 pseudonymous clients from the FlyRank ML Internship dataset, a 90-day trailing search-performance snapshot with a 30-day-vs-previous-30-day decline label.

Method: a Logistic Regression model, evaluated against a transparent staleness-and-CTR-gap rule on an identical, client-grouped held-out split — never a random row split, since 31 of 32 clients would otherwise leak across train and test.

Result: the model improved ROC-AUC from 0.51 to 0.62 and precision@20 from a 51% base rate to 70% over the rule baseline, on the same honest split. A leakage audit confirmed this gain was real: letting one label-derived column back into the feature set pushed AUC to 0.999 — a controlled demonstration of exactly the mistake this design was built to avoid.

What it's for: a ranked, reason-coded queue that tells a content reviewer where to look first and why — decision support for a human, not an automated action.

01 — Introduction

The decision this supports

A content team managing hundreds or thousands of pages can't review every page every month. They need a short, trustworthy list: which pages are most likely declining, and why does the model think so? Get the ranking wrong and reviewers waste time on pages that are fine, or miss pages that are quietly losing visibility.

The unit of analysis here is one page (content item) at one client, over a 90-day trailing window. The output is a rank plus a reason code a human can act on — not a black-box score.

Why ML helps here at all A hand-tuned rule — flag stale, visible, underperforming pages — is a reasonable first pass, but it can only combine a handful of thresholds a person can hold in their head. A model can weigh dozens of signals at once and rank by degree, not just pass/fail. The question this paper actually tests is whether that flexibility earns its complexity on a held-out split, not whether a model can beat a rule in principle.

03 — Methodology

Label, features, baseline, split

Label: a page is "declining" if its impressions over the trailing 30 days fell more than 10% versus the prior 30 days.

Features: 22 total — 18 numeric (traffic volume, log-transformed for skew; position; CTR; content age; freshness; engagement) and 8 categorical (content type, intent, competition level, tier buckets), one-hot encoded.

Baseline: a transparent rule — flag pages that are stale (90+ days since last update), visible (100+ impressions/90d), ranking in a reachable position tier, and underperforming their tier's weighted-CTR benchmark.

Model: Logistic Regression, selected after head-to-head comparison against Random Forest and a shallow Decision Tree — the added flexibility of a forest didn't earn its complexity here.

Validation design: split grouped by client (80/20), never row-level random. Rows from the same client share systematic patterns; a random split lets a model partly memorize per-client baselines instead of learning a transferable signal.

The leakage confession, run live Add back the two columns the label is computed from — trend_pct and the raw 30-day impression window — and AUC jumps from 0.616 to 0.999. Both columns are confirmed removed from the real feature set below.

05 — Limitations

What this cannot claim

  • Not causal. This ranks pages by observed association with decline. It does not claim that refreshing a flagged page will fix it — no experiment was run.
  • One dataset, 32 clients. Not validated across industries or portfolio sizes outside this slice. Re-check before trusting the queue on a very different client base.
  • AUC 0.616 is meaningfully above chance, far from perfect. Expect real false positives and false negatives at these volumes.
  • Label noise at low volume. A 1–2 impression swing can flip the decline label on a very low-traffic page — signal the model can't distinguish from genuine decline.
  • A specific reviewer trap: 51% of the model_only_high_risk reason-code bucket sits at exactly 0% CTR — as likely a tracking gap as a real content problem. This is decision support for a human reviewer, never an autopilot.

Never automate No page is auto-published, auto-edited, merged, pruned, or de-indexed from this score alone. The model's probability is a rank, not a guarantee — treat every flagged page as "needs a human look," not "needs a refresh."
